# The Score That Wasn't the Decision

*A credit-scoring agency argued it hadn't made a decision, only a number. Europe's top court disagreed: if the lender draws strongly enough on that number, producing it was the decision, whether or not a human signs off afterward.*

On 7 December 2023, the Court of Justice of the European Union rejected that division outright. Where a lender draws strongly on a score to decide whether to grant, refuse, or terminate a contract, the court held, generating the score is itself automated individual decision-making under Article 22, regardless of who technically clicks approve or decline afterward. SCHUFA hadn't merely supplied an input. It had made a decision the bank simply carried out. The transparency and safeguard obligations Article 22 imposes now fall on the scoring agency directly, not only on the lender standing at the end of the chain.

## A gap that looked like a division of labour

This is worth setting against the ground an earlier essay in this collection covers. That essay asks whether a human signatory's presence in a process ever meant genuine judgment was exercised. This case assumes the opposite question and finds it doesn't matter as much as everyone assumed: even where a human genuinely does make the final call, a human who draws strongly on an automated score is not exercising the kind of independent judgment that exempts the process from Article 22's reach. The SCHUFA case didn't ask whether the bank's loan officer was paying attention. It established that upstream of the loan officer entirely, a separate party had already made the decision that mattered, while calling it something else.

Before this ruling, a two-party structure like this let each side plausibly point at the other. The scoring agency could say it only provides information; the decision-maker relies on it, but decides. The lender could say it only relies on a score someone else, an expert in scoring, produced; the actual analysis happened elsewhere. Both statements are true and, taken together, left nobody clearly holding Article 22's obligations, exactly the kind of gap this book keeps finding between two parties, each of whom can honestly say the substantive act wasn't fully theirs.

## Why this reaches further than credit scoring

Nothing in the court's reasoning is specific to loans. Any AI system that produces a score, ranking, or recommendation which a downstream party, a hiring manager, an insurer, another piece of software, draws strongly on to make a consequential decision now sits inside the same logic, whether or not a human technically approves the final output. An organisation that assumed it was shielded from automated-decision obligations because "a person makes the actual call" is relying on a distinction the CJEU has already found doesn't hold once that person's call is substantially driven by the score in front of them.

## What actually needs naming

The practical question this raises isn't whether your AI system technically has a human in the loop. It's whether that human draws strongly enough on the system's output that the CJEU's logic would treat the system's step, not just the human's, as the decision. Where that's true, the obligation to be able to explain the score, on request, to the person it affects, sits with whoever generates it, not only with whoever signs off on it last. Very few organisations currently know which of their scoring systems this applies to, because until December 2023 the question hadn't been definitively asked, let alone answered, in a court whose ruling now binds every member state.
