# The Last Human Signature

*A qualified electronic signature carries the same legal weight as a wax seal, and has never certified comprehension, only identity. AI just removed the last friction that was quietly doing comprehension's job for free.*

A qualified electronic signature, produced from a phone in under a second, carries in a European court exactly the legal weight of a wax seal pressed into a document by hand. Both prove the same narrow thing: that a specific person, and no one else, put their mark to a specific text. Neither has ever proven that the person understood what the text said.

That equivalence is older, and stranger, than it looks.

## What the signature was actually built to do

The mark that authenticates a document goes back further than paper. Sumerian cylinder seals, pressed into wet clay around 3500 BC, served the same function a signature serves today: identify the author, deter forgery. Medieval Europe did it with wax. England's Statute of Frauds, in 1677, gave the written and signed mark formal legal teeth, requiring it for wills, contracts and grants specifically to prevent fraudulent claims about what had been agreed.

Every stage after that follows the same pattern, right through to the present one. Under the EU's eIDAS regulation, an electronic signature can be as simple as a typed name; an advanced one must be uniquely linked to the signatory and detect any later tampering with the document; a qualified one adds a certified device and an accredited trust provider, and is the only tier granted automatic legal equivalence to handwriting across the whole union. Each tier asks the same two questions the Sumerians were asking: is this really you, and has the document been altered since. None of them, at any point in five thousand years of refinement, has asked whether the signatory understood what they signed.

That is not an oversight. It was never the job.

## The friction was doing judgment's work for free

If the signature never certified comprehension, why did it feel, for most of its history, like it did?

Because comprehension used to be a byproduct of the mechanics, not a requirement of the law. Signing on paper meant being physically present, holding the actual document, moving a pen across an actual page. None of that guaranteed careful reading. But it made pure inattention harder to sustain, purely as a matter of friction — the same way walking to a shop makes an impulse purchase slightly less impulsive than one-click checkout does. Nobody designed that friction into the legal instrument. It was an accident of inconvenience, and for centuries almost everyone relied on it without noticing they were relying on it at all.

## What happens when the accident goes away

Digital signing removed the friction in one step; AI-assisted document review is removing what little was left. A contract that once took an afternoon to read can now be summarised, flagged, and routed for signature in the time it takes to open an app. The legal instrument hasn't moved an inch — a click carries precisely the weight the wax seal did. What has vanished is the thing that was never actually part of the contract to begin with: the forced, if minimal, contact with the content.

Autonomy's role here is specific, and worth stating carefully, because the obvious version of this argument is wrong. The danger is not that an AI system takes the decision away from the human signatory. It is that the gap between what is being signed and what the signatory could plausibly have evaluated grows faster than any signatory, however diligent, could close it — because system capability compounds and an afternoon's attention does not. The signature was never built to scale against that gap. Nobody has redesigned it since the thing on the other end of the pen stopped being a document a person wrote and became a model output nobody, including its own builders, fully understands.

## Who has no reason to check

It would be convenient to say someone benefits from this, and therefore keeps it this way. The honest version is duller and, for that reason, harder to dismiss: almost nobody involved has any incentive to look closely, and one party has a mild incentive not to.

An insurer needs a natural person for a policy to attach liability to; a board needs someone accountable when a regulator calls; a supervisory authority needs a named individual it can, in principle, fine. None of them requires that signature to represent genuine judgment — only that it exist, be traceable, and hold up as evidence. They would take a genuine signature just as happily as an empty one. What they lack is any mechanism that would ever tell them which they'd got.

The one party with something closer to an actual stake in the fiction is whoever depends on the pipeline moving: an employer for whom a signatory who insists on genuinely evaluating everything they sign is, measured in throughput, a bottleneck. That is a real, if unglamorous, adverse interest — not malice, just an ordinary preference for speed over friction, applied to a place where the friction happened to be doing useful work.

Regulators sit awkwardly outside this pattern, and the piece should say so rather than paper over it. The entire architecture of human-oversight law — competence, training, authority to overrule the system — assumes the signature is, or can be made to be, genuine. Regulators are the one party who would plausibly prefer it real. What none of them has built yet is a way to find out, short of an incident, whether it is.

## The narrower, honest ask

Two comfortable answers don't survive contact with the argument above. Restoring comprehension by force of will is not available — the friction that used to produce it is gone for good, and nostalgia for it is not a policy. Training signatories harder is the other reflex, and it fails for a blunter reason: capability grows faster than any individual's capacity to audit it, no matter how well trained, in the time a sign-off actually takes.

The narrower ask is less satisfying and more defensible: shrink what requires a human signature down to what a human can still, honestly, evaluate in the time available — and stop calling everything outside that boundary oversight. A smaller scope, correctly labelled, is worth more than a large one that quietly isn't what it claims to be.

## The question worth asking before the incident, not after

Seal, ink, or click — the law has never distinguished between them, and on present evidence it isn't about to start. What it has also never done is ask the signatory to prove they understood what they signed.

That was survivable when the friction was doing the asking for free. It stops being survivable exactly at the moment someone, during an incident inquiry, asks a signatory to walk through the judgment behind their signature — and the honest answer is that there wasn't time to have one. The interesting question isn't whether that moment arrives. It's whether anyone is willing to say, now, what the signature currently is, rather than what it has always been assumed to be.

Answering that honestly opens a second question, taken up later in this collection: even a signatory who is genuinely present, genuinely trying to exercise judgment, is not automatically equipped to catch what's wrong. Presence and competence are not the same problem, and conflating them is how oversight quietly becomes a formality without anyone ever deciding it should.
