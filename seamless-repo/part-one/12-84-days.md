# 84 Days

*Sora launched, broke download records, and was discontinued eighty-four days later. Disney, mid-negotiation on a billion-dollar licensing deal, learned about the shutdown less than an hour before everyone else did.*

The detail worth sitting with isn't the shutdown itself. Products get discontinued; that's an ordinary commercial event. It's who found out, and when. OpenAI had been negotiating a licensing deal with Disney reportedly worth a billion dollars, access to more than two hundred characters for use inside Sora. Disney, by most accounts, learned the deal was dead less than an hour before the rest of the world learned the product was too. If a counterparty with that much leverage, that much legal infrastructure, and that much at stake got an hour's notice, the contract terms a small business negotiates with the same vendor were never going to buy meaningfully more warning.

## A risk that rarely shows up as an incident

Most of the essays in this collection describe something going wrong, a breach, a fabricated citation, a control someone forgot was connected to another. A few describe something shifting instead: an earlier piece in this book covers a company that quietly becomes an AI Act provider without anyone deciding it should. This essay belongs with that second group, but pushes further. In that essay, your own status changes underneath you. Here, nothing about your status changes at all. The counterparty's business simply does, on a timeline you never see coming and can't negotiate your way into seeing sooner. The risk here isn't that the vendor's system breaks. It's that the vendor's business decides, for reasons that have nothing to do with your usage or your contract, that continuing to run the product it sold you is no longer worth it, and there is very little in a standard commercial agreement that obligates a vendor to keep a product alive for your convenience.

This sits in an unusual place on most risk registers, which is to say it typically doesn't sit anywhere. Security risk has an owner. Compliance risk has an owner, several, per earlier essays in this book. Vendor discontinuation risk, the possibility that a critical dependency simply stops existing on a timeline you don't control and can't negotiate, rarely has a named owner at all, because it doesn't look like a governance problem until the announcement email arrives.

## What Disney's hour actually proves

It's tempting to read the Disney detail as a story about OpenAI's internal communication failing. It's more useful read as a natural experiment. Disney had every advantage a customer could plausibly have: dedicated legal counsel, a nine-figure commercial relationship, and presumably a contract with termination and notice provisions most companies could never negotiate. None of it bought them more than an hour's warning on the decision that mattered most. That's not a critique of Disney's contract lawyers. It's evidence about the actual ceiling on how much advance warning a customer relationship, however well negotiated, can realistically extract from a vendor whose own survival calculus has changed.

## Naming the risk before the notice arrives

Avoiding vendor dependency entirely isn't realistic advice for an SME building on foundation-model infrastructure it could never build in-house. What's realistic, and currently rare, is naming this risk explicitly rather than letting it hide inside a general sense that "the vendor probably won't just disappear." For each AI vendor a business genuinely depends on: what does export or data portability actually look like today, not in theory, but tested; how long would a real migration take; and who is the named person responsible for triggering that migration the moment a discontinuation notice arrives, rather than the week after, once the shock has worn off and the clock has already been running.

None of this stops a vendor from making the decision Sora's did. It closes the gap between the decision happening and someone in your own organisation actually being ready for it, which, on the evidence of eighty-four days, is currently closer to zero than anyone building on top of these platforms would like to admit.
