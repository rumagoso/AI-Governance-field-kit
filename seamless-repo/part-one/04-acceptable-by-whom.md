# Acceptable, By Whom

*Before any policy told them otherwise, three Samsung engineers defined 'acceptable use' in practice, on a deadline, by pasting confidential code into ChatGPT. The company spent three years and one public leak working out that this was never a question employees should have had to answer alone.*

A ban is not a policy. It is the absence of one, dressed in the clothing of control. It answers "what happened" without answering the harder question a real acceptable-use policy is supposed to answer: not whether a tool is allowed, but which data may go where, under what conditions, and how anyone would know if that boundary had been crossed. Samsung ran on the blunt version for three years. In June 2026, it reversed course entirely and rolled out enterprise ChatGPT access to roughly 125,000 employees, this time with contractual data guarantees attached. The gap between 2023 and 2026 is not a story about a company changing its mind on AI. It is a story about the difference between forbidding a tool and actually governing its use, and how long an organisation can survive on the former before the cost of not having the latter catches up.

## Four frameworks, one requirement, and no one enforcing it consistently

The obligation to have an acceptable-use policy for AI is not a matter of best practice. It is written, independently, into four different frameworks that rarely agree on much else. ISO 27001's Control 5.10 requires a documented, management-approved policy on acceptable use of information assets, with acknowledgment evidence auditors specifically ask for. ISO 42001's A.2.3 goes further for AI specifically, requiring a formal AI policy covering acceptable use, and, critically, evidence that it is enforced rather than merely written down. The EU AI Act's Article 4, in force since 2 August 2025, requires "sufficient AI literacy" among staff and explicitly lists "what the internal usage policies are" as required training content, with no prescribed format and, by the Commission's own account, no clear checklist for compliance. The Cloud Security Alliance's AI Controls Matrix states the same requirement in its own governance domain: organisations must define and document acceptable uses for deployed AI models.

Four frameworks, one convergent answer, and still, per multiple 2026 industry surveys, roughly half of employees admit to entering sensitive company data into public AI tools regardless. The policy usually exists somewhere. What's missing is evidence that anyone encountered it in a form that changed their behaviour.

## The difference between a click and a lesson

An earlier essay in this collection argued that a signature was never proof of comprehension, only of identity, a piece of friction that used to do judgment's work for free. The AUP acknowledgment click is the same mechanism at a smaller scale and a higher volume: a new hire, on day one, alongside a dozen other onboarding documents, clicks "I have read and understood" on a policy they, in the overwhelming majority of cases, have not actually read. ISO 42001's insistence on enforcement evidence rather than documentation is a direct response to this exact gap, an auditor checking for a signed acknowledgment is checking for the same weak signal a court eventually stopped trusting a physical signature to provide.

The honest distinction worth drawing is between three different things that get conflated under "we have an AI policy": a document that exists, a document someone clicked to accept, and a document someone was actually shown, with real examples, in a form that would let them recognise the situation the next time they were in it. Only the third one does the work Article 4's literacy requirement is actually asking for. The first two are what most organisations currently have.

## What Samsung's second attempt got right

The 2026 rollout wasn't just "ChatGPT, but allowed now." It came with tiered data-handling rules, distinguishing what could and couldn't be entered depending on classification, and contractual guarantees about how the vendor would and wouldn't use what employees submitted. That is closer to what Article 4, ISO 42001, and the AICM are actually asking for than the 2023 ban ever was, not because it's more permissive, but because it's specific. A ban requires no judgment from an employee; a real acceptable-use policy requires them to understand a boundary well enough to recognise it in a new situation the policy's authors never anticipated.

Whether that specific 2026 version holds up is a separate question from whether it exists in the right shape. Samsung took three years and one very public leak to work out that "acceptable" was never a question its engineers should have had to answer alone, on a deadline, with no policy in the room. The four frameworks converging on this requirement are, in effect, asking every other organisation to answer it in advance instead, and to write down who actually gets to decide.
