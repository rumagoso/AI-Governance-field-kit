# Nobody Told the Renewal

*Unlike most essays in this collection, this one has no denied claim behind it. The exclusion forms, carrier filings, and approval rates below are real and independently verifiable. What hasn't happened yet, as far as public record shows, is a company finding out the hard way, a claim actually denied on this basis. That is likely a matter of when, not if, given how recently and how broadly these exclusions have been approved, but the essay is honest about arguing from mechanism rather than from casualty.*

For most of the years generative AI tools have been in ordinary business use, organisations carried a comfortable, unstated assumption: whatever went wrong with an AI system, a chatbot giving bad advice, a model leaking data, an automated decision causing harm, would fall under the general commercial liability, cyber, or technology errors-and-omissions policy already sitting in the filing cabinet. Nobody had to check, because nobody had reason to doubt it. The policy had never mentioned AI, which felt like coverage by omission rather than a gap.

That assumption had a name in the industry, silent AI coverage, and it stopped being safe to rely on in January 2026. ISO, whose standard policy language underpins roughly eighty per cent of US commercial property and casualty insurance, introduced two new endorsement forms, CG 40 47 and CG 40 48, that let insurers explicitly exclude claims arising from generative AI use. Within weeks, major carriers, Chubb, Travelers, and Berkshire Hathaway among them, had filed for and received state approval, at a rate above eighty per cent, to add AI exclusions to general liability, D&O, and E&O policies. None of this required a single company's renewal notice to say, in plain language, "your AI risk is no longer covered here." It required only that the exclusion be present in the policy language a renewal quietly carries forward.

## The same mechanism, a different document

An earlier essay in this collection describes what happens when a policy acknowledgment, a signature, a click confirming a document has been read, does the work comprehension used to do for free. Insurance renewal operates on close to the same mechanism, at a scale most SMEs never scrutinise. A commercial policy renewal is, for the overwhelming majority of small and mid-sized organisations, an administrative event: a broker sends a packet, someone with signing authority approves it, and the underlying language is assumed to be substantially the language from last year unless someone flags otherwise. An AI exclusion added at page forty of a renewal packet does not require concealment to go unnoticed. It only requires that nobody was specifically looking for it, which, before January 2026, nobody had reason to.

The distinction worth drawing is the one this book keeps returning to in different forms: a policy existing and a policy actually covering what an organisation assumes it covers are not the same fact, and the gap between them is invisible until the moment a claim tests it.

## What actually fills the gap, and what doesn't

Standalone AI liability coverage exists, and it's growing quickly. HSB launched the first major product aimed specifically at SMEs in March 2026; specialist capacity from reinsurers including Munich Re now offers policies in the two-to-fifty-million-dollar range. This is a genuine, purchasable answer, not a hypothetical one. The problem isn't that no solution exists. It's that buying it requires first knowing the general policy no longer covers what it used to be assumed to cover, and that knowledge currently depends entirely on someone at the organisation having read an endorsement form most brokers aren't yet routinely flagging as a headline change.

## What one afternoon actually buys

This doesn't require an insurance background to fix, only a specific question asked at the next renewal, and the one after that: does our current general liability, cyber, or tech E&O policy contain a generative AI exclusion, by form number if the broker can provide one, and if so, is that gap covered elsewhere or not covered at all. That question takes an afternoon. The alternative is discovering the answer during a claim, at the exact moment the answer costs the most to have gotten wrong.

Nobody rewrote these policies to deceive anyone. The exclusions are filed publicly, approved by state regulators, and, in the plainest sense, entirely legal. They simply arrived the same way most changes to the parts nobody owns arrive: quietly, correctly, and on schedule, while everyone assumed someone else was reading the renewal closely enough to notice.
