# Qualified to Disagree

*A lawyer who genuinely reviewed his own brief still filed six fabricated cases, because reviewing something and being equipped to catch a specific new kind of failure turned out to be two different things, a gap no org chart shows and no seniority fills.*

In June 2023 a New York lawyer named Steven Schwartz filed a federal court brief citing six cases in support of his argument. None of the six existed. He had asked ChatGPT to find supporting precedent, and it had obliged, inventing case names, docket numbers, and quoted holdings with total fluency. Schwartz did not skip the review step. He read the brief before filing it. He simply had no reason to doubt that a citation, formatted like every other citation he had seen in twenty years of practice, might refer to nothing at all. The judge sanctioned him. The case, Mata v. Avianca, has since become the standard reference for AI hallucination in legal practice, usually filed under the heading of oversight failure.

That heading undersells what actually happened. There was oversight. A qualified lawyer read the document before it went to court. What failed was not the presence of a reviewer but the reviewer's competence for this particular task, and the distinction matters more than the case law summaries tend to acknowledge.

## Two different questions

It is worth separating this from a question this series has already asked. An earlier piece in this collection, on the fading meaning of a signature, asked whether the person putting their name to a document was ever exercising judgment at all, or merely completing a ritual that happened to look like judgment from the outside. That question does not need to be resolved here. Assume the opposite: assume Schwartz genuinely intended to review the brief, genuinely believed he was checking it, and would have removed any citation he recognised as fabricated. The question this piece asks is narrower and, in some ways, more uncomfortable. Given a reviewer acting in complete good faith, was he actually equipped to catch the failure in front of him?

He was not, and the reason is specific rather than general. Twenty years of legal practice teaches a lawyer to spot a weak argument, a mischaracterised precedent, a citation that has been overruled. None of that training teaches a lawyer to suspect that a citation, correctly formatted and confidently presented, might simply not exist. That is not a legal skill. It is a skill that did not need to exist before a tool capable of fabricating case law with total fluency became available to anyone with a login.

## What the Act actually asks for

The AI Act's human oversight provisions are usually read as a presence requirement: assign a human, give them the authority to intervene, done. Read more carefully, Article 14 asks for something narrower and harder, that the person exercising oversight have the competence and training necessary to do so meaningfully, not oversight in the abstract, but oversight matched to the specific failure modes of the specific system in front of them. A radiologist reviewing an AI-flagged scan and a lawyer reviewing an AI-drafted brief are not exercising the same competence merely because both hold a professional qualification. The competence that matters is calibrated to what the tool in front of them is actually capable of getting wrong, and that competence does not transfer from a general credential the way organisations tend to assume it does.

This is where most human oversight programmes quietly fail the requirement without anyone noticing. A firm assigns a senior associate to review AI-drafted work because seniority reads as competence on an org chart. Seniority in the underlying discipline is not the same thing as trained suspicion of a specific, novel failure mode. Nobody taught Schwartz to distrust a well-formatted citation, because until early 2023 there was no reason to.

## The narrower ask

The honest response is not more oversight, and not more senior reviewers. It is a specific, teachable competence: familiarity with how the particular AI system in use actually fails, not AI failure in the abstract, but the failure modes of this tool, in this workflow, doing this kind of work. That competence can be built. It requires naming, in writing, what a reviewer is actually being asked to catch, and confirming, before deployment rather than after an incident, that the person in the reviewing seat has been shown what that specific failure looks like.

Schwartz was qualified to practise law. He was not qualified to disagree with a citation that had never existed in the first place, because nobody had ever needed to be.
